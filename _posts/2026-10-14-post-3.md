*Part 3 of Building a Cheaper Critic · 30 October 2026*

Imagine measuring the same quantity with two instruments whose readings are almost identical. Having both readings sounds better than having one. But if we ask a regression model to assign each instrument its own importance, tiny differences can produce surprisingly large changes in the fitted weights.

Transformer features can present a similar problem. A prefix has many hidden coordinates, and several layers may repeat related information. More coordinates can help prediction, but they can also make fitting expensive and sensitive.

A useful representation must do two jobs: retain information about success and organize it into coordinates that a small head can use.

This is the motivation behind designs such as SLICE: learn a compact description first, then fit a simple value head. The argument for that separation is geometric and computational; whether a particular representation succeeds remains an empirical question.

## 1. Read only the prefix that exists

At a state $$s$$, collect selected transformer block outputs at the last available token:

$$
x(s)=[h_{\ell_1}(s);\ldots;h_{\ell_K}(s)]\in\mathbb R^D.
$$

These are different layers describing the same text, not glimpses of future reasoning. Features for “Twelve divided by” must not include the next token “three.” Causal feature extraction is essential even if a complete answer is available during training.

Per-layer RMS normalization can control differences in activation magnitude:

$$
\widetilde h=\frac{h}{\sqrt{D_h^{-1}\sum_jh_j^2+\delta}}.
$$

Here $$\delta$$ is a numerical stabilizer. This normalization controls each vector's scale. It does not decorrelate its coordinates or guarantee a well-conditioned regression matrix.

## 2. Compression should preserve the right information

PCA finds directions that preserve variance under a linear reconstruction objective. But a feature that varies strongly need not predict success. Answer length might account for more variation than a subtle distinction between valid and invalid arithmetic.

A supervised bottleneck instead learns features useful for an outcome target:

$$
z(s)=\operatorname{ReLU}(W\widetilde x(s)+b),
\qquad z(s)\in\mathbb R^m,\quad m<D.
$$

Attach a temporary predictor $$p(s)=\sigma(a^\top z(s)+c)$$ and fit it using binary cross-entropy. After training, freeze the bottleneck and replace the temporary readout with a separately fitted Ridge head.

For a fixed policy, let $$v=V^\pi(s)$$. The expected binary cross-entropy at that state is

$$
-v\log p-(1-v)\log(1-p).
$$

Its minimum is at $$p=v$$. This gives outcome supervision a clear purpose: learn coordinates that help predict expected success. Finite data, restricted models and optimization still determine how closely we approach that ideal.

## 3. Compactness and sparsity are different choices

A narrow vector may still have every coordinate active. An activation penalty can encourage selective use:

$$
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{prediction}}
+\beta\frac1{Nm}\sum_{i,j}|z_{ij}|.
$$

ReLU outputs are nonnegative, so the added term is proportional to mean activation. Width determines how many coordinates exist; the penalty discourages using large total activation. Neither guarantees that the coordinates are independent or meaningful.

Normalizing projection rows blocks one route for shrinking the projection while enlarging the readout. It does not eliminate every scaling freedom involving biases and readout weights. Sparsity therefore needs its own controls: compare the same bottleneck with and without the activation penalty.

Likewise, sparse activations do not imply a sparse weight matrix or faster dense matrix multiplication. Those are separate implementation questions.

## 4. Conditioning measures sensitivity, not intelligence

For a nonsingular system $$Mw=b$$, the two-norm condition number is

$$
\kappa_2(M)=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(M)}{\sigma_{\min}(M)}.
$$

A large value means some directions are much more weakly constrained than others. Perturbations from rounding, noisy labels or slightly changed data can then have a large effect in sensitive directions. This is a worst-case sensitivity measure, not a prediction-error score.

Consider a design matrix whose two feature columns are nearly duplicates:

$$
X=[u,\ u+\eta v],\qquad |\eta|\ll1.
$$

Increasing one coefficient while decreasing the other changes predictions only slightly on the observed data. The model has little evidence for distinguishing their individual effects.

For a full-column-rank design, forming the unregularized Gram matrix squares its condition number:

$$
\kappa_2(X^\top X)=\kappa_2(X)^2.
$$

This is one reason to care about the geometry of the features before accumulating their outer products.

## 5. How can learned compression help?

A useful compressor can remove redundant coordinates, control scale and preserve directions relevant to the target. That can make the smaller Ridge system better conditioned than a wider layer readout.

The word **can** matters. Smaller matrices are not automatically better conditioned. A compressor can also create duplicate features, nearly inactive directions or poorly balanced scales. Unit-length projection rows are not orthogonal rows; selective activations are not independent activations.

Even better-conditioned weights do not guarantee lower test error. A perfectly stable representation that discards arithmetic information can predict badly. Conversely, correlated features may yield useful predictions while their individual coefficients remain unstable.

| Question | Appropriate measurement |
|---|---|
| Does the representation predict success? | Held-out value or outcome error |
| Is the fitted system sensitive? | Condition number of the system actually solved |
| Is the representation economical? | Extraction, storage and fitting costs |

These measurements describe different strengths. One cannot stand in for the others.

## 6. Ridge changes the spectrum too

Let the Gram matrix have eigenvalues $$\mu_{\max}\geq\mu_{\min}\geq0$$. Penalizing every coordinate with $$\epsilon I$$ gives

$$
\kappa_2(G+\epsilon I)
=\frac{\mu_{\max}+\epsilon}{\mu_{\min}+\epsilon}.
$$

For an illustrative diagonal matrix with entries 10,000 and 1, the condition number is 10,000. Adding $$9I$$ changes it to $$10{,}009/10\approx1{,}001$$. The system is less sensitive, but its fitted solution has changed too.

Large regularization can improve conditioning while shrinking predictions too much. A tiny regularizer may barely affect a large accumulated Gram matrix. Report the spectrum or condition number alongside the regularization and data-scaling convention, then choose predictive trade-offs on validation data.

## 7. Freeze what the statistics depend on

Once features are learned, define $$\phi(s)=[z(s);1]$$ and accumulate Ridge or LSTD statistics as described in [Article 2]({% post_url 2026-10-14-post-2 %}). Keep the encoder, bottleneck and preprocessing fixed while reusing those statistics.

For transfer to another task, freezing the representation and refitting a small head is an attractive low-data option. It works only if useful distinctions survive the change in tasks or policies. A compact representation can transfer well without being universally best, especially when abundant target data allows other features to catch up.

The design goal is therefore more precise than “make the vectors smaller”: preserve useful distinctions while reducing the expense and sensitivity of fitting them.
