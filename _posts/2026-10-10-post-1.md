*Part 1 of Building a Cheaper Critic*

Suppose a language model is asked a small question:

> I have five avocados and buy four more. Each serving of guacamole needs three avocados. How many servings can I make?

It writes, “Five plus four is nine. Nine divided by three is three.” A verifier accepts the final answer, and the model receives a reward of one.

What should it learn? The reward arrives once, but the model made many decisions. Some established the arithmetic; others supplied punctuation. The problem of turning one outcome into useful feedback for those decisions is called **credit assignment**.

Before building a cheaper critic, we need to understand the job we are asking it to do.

## 1. Treat writing as a sequence of decisions

A policy is a distribution over actions. For a language model, write it as $$\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$$: the probability of the next token, given the text so far.

| Quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| State $$s_t$$ | Question and all answer tokens generated so far |
| Action $$a_t$$ | The next token |
| Next state $$s_{t+1}$$ | The same text with that token appended |
| Return $$R$$ | Final verified outcome, here zero or one |

Appending a chosen token is deterministic. Uncertainty comes from which tokens the policy will choose afterward. Include the remaining generation budget and stopping rules when they affect the outcome: a model with ten tokens left faces a different problem from one with a thousand.

In our running example, intermediate rewards are zero, and the final transition receives the outcome reward. We use discount $$\gamma=1$$, so a successful answer is not worth less merely because it took longer.

## 2. A value predicts what happens next

Stop after “Five plus four is nine.” The critic asks:

> If this policy continues from this exact prefix, how likely is verified success?

For binary terminal rewards,

$$
V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E[R\mid s,\pi]
=P(\text{verified success}\mid s,\pi).
$$

The superscript matters. Two models can finish the same prefix with different success probabilities. Changing the continuation policy changes the value we want to predict.

Value also differs from the correctness of the last sentence. A correct intermediate calculation can be followed by a mistake. An incorrect calculation can be repaired. And a final-answer verifier may accept an answer without establishing that every step of its explanation is valid.

The critic predicts the outcome defined by the verifier, under a particular policy. That is already a useful task, but a narrower one than certifying reasoning.

## 3. One outcome is evidence, not a probability

Suppose we continue the same prefix independently eight times and six attempts succeed. A Monte Carlo estimate is

$$
\widehat V_{\mathrm{MC},K}(s)=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_k,
\qquad \widehat V_{\mathrm{MC},8}(s)=0.75.
$$

One successful continuation would instead give target one. That is a valid observed return, but it does not establish that success is certain. A learned critic pools evidence across prefixes to predict the expected return.

This explains why all prefixes of one completed answer can receive the same observed label. They share an outcome, not necessarily the same true value. They are also correlated examples, not independent trials.

## 4. Compare the chosen action with expectations

An advantage asks how good an action is relative to the policy's usual behavior:

$$
A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s).
$$

Here $$Q^\pi(s,a)$$ means expected return after choosing $$a$$ and then continuing with $$\pi$$. For a nonterminal token with no immediate reward, deterministic text transitions and $$\gamma=1$$ give

$$
A^\pi(s,a)=V^\pi(s')-V^\pi(s).
$$

If choosing a token moves success probability from 0.4 to 0.6, its advantage is 0.2. This is an action's expected benefit under the specified continuation policy, not proof that a particular successful answer depended on that token.

At termination, the immediate reward must be included. The terminal state's value is zero because no future rewards remain.

## 5. Practical advantages combine several pieces of evidence

With an approximate critic, the one-step temporal-difference residual is

$$
\delta_t=r_t+\gamma\widehat V(s_{t+1})-\widehat V(s_t).
$$

Generalized Advantage Estimation combines residuals along the sampled answer:

$$
\widehat A_t=\delta_t+\gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}\widehat A_{t+1}.
$$

For an illustrative final transition with value 0.4, success gives residual $$1-0.4=0.6$$; failure gives $$0-0.4=-0.4$$. Earlier states receive information through the recursion. The parameter $$\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}$$ controls how far that sampled evidence propagates. These are noisy learning signals, and approximate values introduce a bias–variance trade-off. [GAE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438)

An important consequence follows. A critic can be somewhat wrong about both neighboring values while getting their difference right. It can also predict individual values reasonably well while missing the small change that makes one action better than another.

## 6. The actor and critic have different jobs

The actor changes token probabilities using advantages. PPO moderates those changes through a clipped objective. With probability ratio

$$
\rho_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)},
$$

the objective is

$$
\mathbb E_t\!\left[\min\left(
\rho_t\widehat A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-c,1+c)\widehat A_t
\right)\right].
$$

Clipping limits the incentive to move an action's probability farther in an already favorable direction. It is not a hard bound on how much the whole policy changes. [PPO paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)

Nothing in this objective requires the critic to be another full transformer. It requires useful advantages. A frozen encoder and a small fitted head might supply them, provided the features retain the information needed to predict future success.

That gives us two questions to keep separate: does the critic predict values well, and do its predictions help the actor learn? We will return to both after understanding how to fit the head.
