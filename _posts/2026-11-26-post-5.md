*Part 5 of Building a Cheaper Critic · 26 November 2026*

Suppose two critics make similar predictions, but one has a much smaller fitted head. Is the smaller critic better?

Perhaps. We still need to know what the predictions mean, where they fail, what fitting costs and whether the resulting advantages help learning. A single accuracy number cannot answer all four questions.

The previous articles separate the design into parts: a policy supplies experience, frozen features describe prefixes, a head predicts values, and a choice of token or chunk boundaries determines where it is fitted. This final article asks how to test those choices without confusing their effects.

## 1. Decide which value function is being tested

Recall that

$$
V^\pi(s)=P(\text{verified success}\mid s,\pi).
$$

There are two legitimate comparisons:

| Design | What it answers |
|---|---|
| One frozen actor, several critics, shared data and references | Which estimator predicts this policy's values best? |
| Several independently trained actors with their own critics | How accurately does each system estimate its own actor's values? |

Matched prefixes do not make distinct actors the same policy. If one actor succeeds 90% of the time from a prefix and another succeeds 60%, their correct targets differ. Their scatter plots can share states while having different horizontal coordinates.

For an isolated representation or readout experiment, freeze the actor and share the targets. For a comparison of complete training methods, let each actor learn, then evaluate against its own continuation distribution. Explain which question the experiment answers.

## 2. Build a reference that matches the task

At a fixed prefix, sample $$K$$ independent continuations under the target policy and average the verified rewards:

$$
\widehat V_{\mathrm{MC},K}(s)=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_k.
$$

For binary rewards with success probability $$v$$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat V_{\mathrm{MC},K}\mid s)=\frac{v(1-v)}K.
$$

This is an estimate, not exact ground truth. Increasing $$K$$ reduces sampling noise; it does not fix an imperfect verifier or a mismatched generation budget. Repeated generations should use the same policy, sampling settings and stopping rules that define the value being evaluated.

Separate training outcomes from evaluation references. If a small MC estimator is compared with a larger reference, use disjoint samples so their shared randomness does not make agreement artificially easy.

## 3. Read the scatter plot before ranking the models

Put the MC reference on the horizontal axis and the critic prediction on the vertical axis. The dashed line $$y=x$$ is the ideal agreement line, not a line fitted to the observations.

Points above it overpredict success; points below it underpredict. A cloud compressed toward the middle often overpredicts difficult states and underpredicts easy ones. High correlation alone does not establish agreement: predictions $$\widehat V=V+0.2$$ can track changes perfectly while remaining systematically wrong.

Mean absolute error summarizes vertical distance:

$$
\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1N\sum_i
|\widehat V(s_i)-\widehat V_{\mathrm{MC},K}(s_i)|.
$$

RMSE gives greater weight to large errors. If questions contribute different numbers of prefixes, first average errors within each question, then average over questions. Otherwise verbose answers can dominate the headline result.

## 4. Outcome prediction is a related, different test

Sometimes the held-out label is one observed outcome $$R$$ rather than an MC mean. For a fixed prediction $$p$$ and binary success probability $$v$$,

$$
\mathbb E[(p-R)^2\mid s]=(p-v)^2+v(1-v).
$$

The first term is value-estimation error. The second is variability in the outcome itself. This identity explains why a predictor cannot generally drive held-out outcome squared error to zero even when it knows the true success probability.

For predictions constrained to probabilities, this squared-error metric is the Brier score. Raw linear outputs can lie outside $$[0,1]$$. State whether they were clipped or calibrated, and report raw and adjusted versions separately when both are relevant.

Classification accuracy at threshold 0.5 answers yet another question. Predictions 0.51 and 0.99 receive the same classification, despite making very different probability claims.

## 5. Test the differences that drive decisions

For a nonterminal action with zero immediate reward and $$\gamma=1$$,

$$
A^\pi(s,a)=V^\pi(s')-V^\pi(s).
$$

Suppose true values are 0.4 and 0.6. A critic predicting 0.5 and 0.7 gets both values wrong by 0.1 but gets their difference exactly right. A critic predicting 0.5 and 0.5 has the same value MAE on this pair and misses the improvement entirely.

This motivates advantage error, sign agreement and ranking alternative next steps. Individual-token changes can be small, so uncertain reference signs deserve care. Token-level differences, chunk-level differences and the actual GAE training signal should be labeled separately.

GRPO supplies an instructive contrast. Its outcome-supervised signal centers and scales each answer's reward within a prompt group, then shares that signal across the answer's tokens. It is not a learned prefix value difference. Comparing its usefulness requires respecting that difference in construction and scale. [DeepSeekMath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)

## 6. Look for failures hidden by the average

A single MAE can hide accurate prompt-only predictions and weak later-state predictions. Evaluate early, middle and late reasoning positions, showing how many questions each contains. Prefer matched question coverage when interpreting a trend across positions.

If later-state errors are large, collect more diverse later prefixes, improve their targets, or give them greater fitting weight:

$$
\min_w\ \frac1N\sum_i\alpha_i
(w^\top\phi_i-\widehat V_{\mathrm{MC}}(s_i))^2
+\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2.
$$

Normalize weights to a stated convention and retain early-state evaluation. More late data and larger late weights both increase late influence; test them separately before combining them. Compare frozen features with retrained features using identical head-fitting examples to isolate representation learning.

Better late predictions would support this targeted strategy. They would not, by themselves, establish better actor learning.

## 7. A cheap component is not necessarily a cheap system

Measure feature extraction, head accumulation, solving and actor updates separately. For memory, distinguish head parameters, cached feature storage, live tensor allocations and GPU-wide reserved memory. A compact head can save substantially in one category while barely changing another.

A controlled timing experiment fixes the workload and hardware, repeats methods in varied order and separates warm-up from measurement. A learning experiment instead lets methods generate different answers; compare performance against generated tokens and actual compute as well as update count.

To ask how quickly a method reaches target quality $$q$$, define

$$
\tau(q)=\inf\{t:\operatorname{quality}(t)\geq q\}.
$$

Specify the evaluation rule and report methods that never reach the target. A final accuracy paired with a separate microbenchmark is useful context, but not a measured time-to-quality curve.

## 8. Make the evidence fit the claim

Split by question, not neighboring prefixes. Use validation data to choose settings and a held-out test set for the locked comparison. Repeat model training across seeds, and distinguish seed variation from uncertainty across evaluation questions. Prefixes from one answer should not masquerade as independent experiments.

For actor training, also guard against fitting a baseline to the very outcome it immediately judges. Using an earlier head or suitable cross-fitting separates that outcome from its own baseline fit. Representation learning needs the same attention to label reuse. These safeguards address fitting dependence, not all approximation error or policy drift.

The conceptual loop is small enough to write down:

```text
FIX the encoder, compressor and preprocessing
COLLECT fresh answers and their verified outcomes
UPDATE head statistics, keeping baseline-fit dependence controlled
COMPUTE detached values and advantages for actor learning
UPDATE the actor using its fresh sampled actions
EVALUATE prediction quality, task performance and total cost separately
```

A smaller head with similar value error supports compact prediction. A lower condition number supports reduced numerical sensitivity of the fitted system. Fewer fitted states support lower accumulation work. Stronger actor learning and lower total cost require their own measurements.

These conclusions can reinforce one another without being interchangeable. Together, they let us ask a practical research question: how much representation, fitting and experience do we actually need for a critic that is useful?
