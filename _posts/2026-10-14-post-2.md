*Part 2 of Building a Cheaper Critic · 14 October 2026*

Imagine keeping a notebook of every prefix a language model has visited and whether its answer eventually succeeded. To update a predictor, we could reread those pages and run gradient descent.

For a linear head, there is another option. Each page can contribute to a matrix and a vector. Together, these summaries contain everything needed for a particular least-squares fit. New experience changes the summaries; a linear solve changes the weights.

The appeal is not that prediction becomes free. It is that fitting can become a small, explicit calculation.

## 1. Linear in the weights does not mean simple in the text

Let a fixed encoder map a prefix to features $$z(s)$$. Append an intercept:

$$
\phi(s)=[z(s);1],\qquad \widehat V_w(s)=w^\top\phi(s).
$$

The encoder can be a large nonlinear network. The restriction is on the final readout: its weights combine existing coordinates linearly.

Think of the encoder as preparing measurements and the head as learning how to combine them. If the measurements expose the distinction between sound and confused arithmetic, a weighted sum may work well. If they hide it, a more accurate linear solve cannot recover missing information.

## 2. Derive Ridge from the prediction error

For training pairs $$(\phi_i,y_i)$$, consider

$$
\mathcal L(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w^\top\phi_i-y_i)^2
+\epsilon\lVert w\rVert_2^2.
$$

Here $$y_i$$ may be one observed return or an average of independent returns from that prefix. The coefficient $$\epsilon>0$$ discourages large weights. In this formulation it penalizes the intercept too; excluding the intercept would be a different, valid convention.

Differentiating and setting the gradient to zero gives

$$
\left(\sum_i\phi_i\phi_i^\top+\epsilon I\right)w
=\sum_i\phi_i y_i.
$$

Define

$$
G=\sum_i\phi_i\phi_i^\top,\qquad b=\sum_i\phi_i y_i.
$$

Then solve $$(G+\epsilon I)w=b$$. We normally use a numerical solver rather than explicitly forming the matrix inverse.

## 3. What do the summaries remember?

For a toy feature vector $$\phi=(2,1)^\top$$ and outcome $$y=1$$, one observation contributes

$$
\phi\phi^\top=
\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \phi y=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The matrix records which coordinates occur together. The vector records how they occur alongside the target. A failure with the same features adds the same matrix but a zero vector. Combining successes and failures moves the fitted value toward their observed frequency.

For another batch, add its matrix and vector. Do not add separately fitted weight vectors: in general, $$w_1+w_2$$ is not the solution for the combined data.

These are **sufficient statistics for this fitting objective**. They do not preserve every fact about the original examples, and cannot answer arbitrary new questions about them.

## 4. Accumulation needs fixed coordinates

Suppose coordinate seven means one thing today and another tomorrow. Adding their outer products no longer describes a single feature map.

To accumulate unchanged statistics, keep the encoder, compressor, centering and scaling fixed. If the representation changes, generally re-extract the old features or rebuild the statistics from a new dataset. A justified coordinate transformation can sometimes preserve a linear system; an arbitrary nonlinear retraining cannot.

There is a separate issue when the actor changes. Yesterday's targets describe yesterday's continuation policy. Fixed coordinates do not make those targets current. Historical accumulation trades more experience against possible policy mismatch.

## 5. Epsilon has meaning only alongside the objective

Our objective used a **sum** of squared errors. Dividing its normal equation by $$N$$ gives

$$
\left(\frac GN+\frac\epsilon N I\right)w=\frac bN.
$$

Thus a fixed $$\epsilon$$ becomes smaller relative to accumulating data. If instead the objective is mean squared error plus $$\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$$, the summed system uses $$G+N\lambda I$$.

This is why “we used regularization 0.01” is incomplete without the normalization convention. Feature scaling matters too: rescaling a coordinate changes the penalty associated with using it. Select regularization using validation data, under the same conventions used for fitting.

## 6. LSTD fits a different relationship

Ridge directly fits returns. Least-squares temporal-difference learning, or LSTD, instead seeks consistency with TD updates. With consecutive features and a fixed policy, the empirical TD(0) fixed-point equation is

$$
\sum_t\phi_t\left[r_t+\gamma w^\top\phi_{t+1}-w^\top\phi_t\right]=0.
$$

Rearranging yields

$$
A=\sum_t\phi_t(\phi_t-\gamma\phi_{t+1})^\top,
\qquad b=\sum_t\phi_t r_t,\qquad Aw=b.
$$

Unlike Ridge's Gram matrix, $$A$$ need not be symmetric. Adding $$\epsilon I$$ here is a modification of the TD system; it is not automatically the same operation as minimizing Ridge's prediction objective. [Original LSTD paper](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~pabbeel/cs287-fa09/readings/BradtkeBarto-lstd-1996.pdf)

Eligibility traces replace the left-hand feature by

$$
e_t=\gamma\lambda_c e_{t-1}+\phi_t.
$$

Then accumulate $$e_t(\phi_t-\gamma\phi_{t+1})^\top$$ and $$e_t r_t$$. Reset the trace between answers. At true termination, the next feature is zero, including its intercept. An artificial cutoff requires an explicit decision about whether the task actually terminates there.

## 7. When can the two fits agree?

For complete episodes with $$\gamma=1$$, $$\lambda_c=1$$ and zero terminal features, expanding the traces makes feature differences telescope:

$$
A=\sum_i\phi_i\left[\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}(\phi_t-\phi_{t+1})\right]^\top
=\sum_i\phi_i\phi_i^\top.
$$

The corresponding vector is $$b=\sum_i\phi_i G_i$$, where $$G_i$$ is the observed return from state $$i$$. Under these assumptions and matching regularization, LSTD(1) agrees with outcome regression. For smaller traces or different weighting, that identity need not hold.

Neither formulation guarantees an exact value function. A tiny solve residual says the equations were solved accurately; held-out predictions tell us whether those equations produced something useful.

## 8. Small heads still need economical features

For $$m$$ coordinates, dense statistics use $$O(m^2)$$ storage. Forming them from $$N$$ examples costs roughly $$O(Nm^2)$$; a conventional dense solve costs $$O(m^3)$$.

Those costs explain the interest in compact representations. They also explain why an excessively wide readout can erase the attraction of avoiding neural critic backpropagation. The encoder's forward pass remains part of the budget.
